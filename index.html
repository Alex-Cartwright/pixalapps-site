Pixal

A quiet pixel-art game for iPhone. You share a cabin with a small creature. There is nothing to win and nothing to lose — it potters about, forms opinions, and gets on with its day whether or not you are watching.

In development

It listens to what you're playing

Put music on — anything, in any app — and he notices. Pixal checks whether something else is playing every few seconds while it's open, so he's never more than about five seconds behind your thumb.

What he does about it is up to him. He doesn't dance on cue. When music starts he decides whether to join: a roll against a chance built out of his mood, the hour, and what he's in the middle of. Being well rested makes him likelier. An hour he's fond of makes him likelier still. Catching him mid-task at a piece of furniture makes him much less likely. If he passes, he carries on pottering and quietly reconsiders every ten minutes or so — a few times, then he lets it go.

When he does join and he owns the gear, he goes and fetches a record first. The design note in the source puts it better than a feature list can: he is a pet, not a mirror — taste, mood and the hour decide it, and your streaks never do.

A world that keeps its own time

The world runs on the real clock and the real season. Dawn, day, dusk and night are genuinely different places to be, and spring, summer, autumn and winter change both what you see and what there is to do.

The sky is built, not painted

The sky is nine flat colours arranged as dithered horizontal bands. The structure stays; the nine colours are swapped for the conditions of the moment. Dawn, dusk and night are fixed moods. Daytime belongs to the season. Weather then washes over whichever palette was chosen — clear, cloud, rain, wind, fog and snow tint the sky rather than replacing it.

The ground is generated as you reach it

The trail is produced a column at a time as the world comes into view — scrub, dirt, dressing and fringe, every ink keyed to the sky's palette so the ground always belongs to the weather above it. It doesn't loop, and it doesn't repeat.

Light you can see the edges of

Every frame, the lighting is drawn into its own layer: the ambient light for the time of day, dimmed by whatever grey the weather has brought, and then each lamp and the hearth as a stack of hard-edged discs. That layer can only darken the scene, so lit things get a second pass to bloom back through it. There are no soft gradients anywhere — seven banded discs per light, every edge stepping along the pixel grid. The banding is the point, not a limitation.

Pixal is built with React Native, Expo and Skia, with a pixel-art renderer written for this game rather than an off-the-shelf engine.

He works out what he likes

He is not waiting for instructions. He wanders, settles, naps, eats, takes an interest in things and has his own opinion about where to be.

Every time he settles somewhere, the moment is scored. Was the fire lit? Was there a record on? Was there rain at the glass? Had you just said hello? Was he full? That score is learned into a fondness for that particular piece of furniture, and the fondness feeds straight back into how he chooses where to go next — so a chair he loves genuinely pulls him across the room.

Opinions can be negative as well as positive, and they fade if nothing keeps them alive — the memory has a half-life of about a fortnight. Over a week or two the room stops being interchangeable props and becomes his: the armchair by the fire, the desk by the window, the bed he never much liked.

Keeping house

Cooking, and a book he writes himself

You cook together from what's actually in the larder. Dishes aren't a list handed to you at the start — the recipe book is where a dish goes once he recognises it, and until he's tasted something its page stays unnamed: something that crackles · something that spreads · something golden. Recipes he knows are plotted on a taste map, sharp to sweet across, light and cool to heavy down, so his palate is something you can actually look at.

The garden

Six beds by the front door, sown and tended through the year. Fourteen crops, each with the seasons it will actually take in — peas, broad beans and strawberries in the spring and summer; tomatoes and courgettes in high summer; pumpkins in autumn; leeks, kale and parsnips carrying through the winter; garlic going in before the cold. Every bed moves through five visible stages — sown, sprout, young, budding, ripe — and a ripe bed waits for you rather than spoiling. A frost will take whatever is still in the ground.

Going out

He takes himself off for the day along named routes with real landmarks and a turnaround point, and comes back with what he found. Shorter trips you do together — fishing, foraging, a run — are minutes rather than hours.

What's out there depends on when you go. The field book fills with finds that only appear under the right combination of season, sky and hour, so a thing you've never seen may simply be waiting for the right afternoon.

And sometimes he doesn't bring home a thing — he brings home a place. On the beat where he'd normally settle at his destination, there's a chance he keeps going because there's something past it. The odds climb the longer it's been since the last one, so a dry run is never wasted. A place he finds belongs to you both permanently, and it works twice over: somewhere new to spend a whole day, and somewhere you can go together for an afternoon.

A micropet of your own

Your real step count hatches something. Walk enough and an egg opens into a small creature of its own — which one is down to chance — and it grows as you keep moving.
